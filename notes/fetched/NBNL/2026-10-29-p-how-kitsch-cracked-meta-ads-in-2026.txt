How Kitsch cracked Meta Ads in 2026
A case study on how to get upstream of the problem.
Oct 29, 2026
Bryan Bumgardner
This week, in data
Chaos erupts as we near midterms. Since around mid-summer, $2.1 billion in ad spend has flowed into these ad platforms, and this weekend is guaranteed to be the pinnacle.
According to the New York Times
, this spend isn’t widespread, however. It’s localized to battleground states and specific Senate competitions, like we’ve seen in Michigan, Texas and Ohio.
According to local reporting,
more than $560 million has been spent
between parties in the battle for one Texas Senate seat.
That’s the most expensive Senate election cycle in American history.
The New York Times insights lay the previous weeks of this newsletter into clearer context: the ad spend has been shifting around wildly based on polling.
For example, Republicans pulled back in Georgia and North Carolina, apparently conceding those elections. Funny, political spenders seem to be optimizing their ad spend quite similar to us.
And you already know these political ads capture a lot of “mindshare.” These political ads are often intense and oppressive, which in my experience can make other ads around these less effective. Nobody can think or talk about anything except the election right now.
Plus this is the first real election cycle where campaigns have embraced AI tools,
resulting in hardcore ads like this one.
The result is wildly variable performance across geos, categories, and demographics, throwing our weekly charts into a tailspin.
Thankfully, Election Day is next week, and once Americans have voted we can get back into the business of buying stuff. Be prepared to see crazy shifting performance in your accounts after November 3rd. Adjust accordingly.
Now, on to an interesting story of arbitrage.
Kitsch: a story of innovation
Those of you who follow real ecommerce winners know about
Kitsch
. The woman- and minority-owned brand is legendary for their satin pillowcases (I have four of them), heatless curlers and shampoo bars.
Their team
has relied on Northbeam
for years as their source of truth. Now they rely on
Northbeam Apex
to help them squeeze even more performance out of Meta.
The problem
Kitsch relies on Northbeam as its source of truth across every channel, Meta included. It set up its attribution to measure growth, and for Kitsch, that means first-time buyers.
This works, but eventually you reach a limit with Meta: although Kitsch was making ad experiment decisions based on Northbeam’s performance data, Meta’s ad algorithms were still optimizing based on Meta’s in-platform numbers.
Meta was playing by different rules. With standard attribution settings, Meta decided for itself which purchases its ads deserved credit for, then optimized for conversions in general. A first-time buyer and someone coming back for a second purchase looked the same.
Kitsch was making budget calls in Northbeam, while Meta chased a different definition of success.
This resulted in a disconnect. The Kitsch team wanted more power and control over their Meta Ads algorithms, so they turned to Northbeam Apex.
The solution
Kitsch turned on Northbeam Apex, which feeds Northbeam's attribution data into Meta's delivery system with the click of a button. Using Meta's Custom Attribution setting, the algorithm could finally optimize toward what Kitsch cared about: new customers.
Think of this like giving Meta the “context” that Northbeam has, which permits Meta to better optimize using the Northbeam numbers you’re using every day.
To prove it worked, Kitsch ran a three-week A/B test of Apex-optimized delivery against Meta's standard attribution, and measured the results in Northbeam.
The impact
The A/B test showed that the Apex-empowered campaigns dramatically outperformed the baseline:
+8.2% New-Customer ROAS
−8.7% Cost Per New-Customer Order
This is a marked improvement in performance with just the press of a few buttons. Pretty great.
